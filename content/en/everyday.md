## A dinner on your own

You walk into a noodle shop alone, which in Korea is ordinary enough to have a name, *honbap*. Water is free, sometimes from a dispenser by the wall. When you are ready to order you press the call button on the table instead of waving; it may be louder than you expect. At the end you take the slip to the counter and pay what the menu said, tax and service already in it. Our pick for the ending scene: walking out after paying the listed price, with nothing left to work out.

## Things that surprise first-timers

| Situation | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The bill arrives and you wonder about a tip | Pay the listed price, nothing more | Tipping is not expected, and menu prices must already include tax and service |
| You want Korean BBQ on your own | Ask before you sit, or pick a place with single servings | Some restaurants ask solo diners to order two servings |
| No one comes to your table | Press the call button on the table | Every table has one; servers wait for it |
| You need help in English at 11 p.m. | Call 1330 (until midnight); for emergencies 112 or 119 | 1330 interprets in 8 languages; 112 and 119 run 24 hours |

Our three that matter most: no tipping, the 1330 number, and the plug adapter. Everything else on this page is good manners rather than a problem you need to solve in advance.

## Tipping: don't

The Korea Tourism Organization (KTO) says it plainly: "Tipping is not required or expected in Korea." Since 2013, the Food Sanitation Act has required restaurants to show final prices that include tax and service charges, so what you see on the menu is what you pay.

A few cafés have put out tip jars or added tip buttons to tablets in recent years, and Korean customers mostly pushed back. A tip jar is optional. Taxis run on the meter and do not expect tips either, according to KTO.

## Eating alone

Eating alone has a name in Korean, *honbap*, and it is ordinary. More than a third of Korean households are one person (36.1% in 2024, National Data Office), and Statistics Korea's 2024 time-use survey found roughly a quarter of lunches and dinners were eaten alone. Noodle shops, gukbap (rice soup) places, kimbap shops, food courts and convenience stores are all easy solo options.

The exception is anything cooked at the table for sharing. Some grill-your-own BBQ and hot-pot places ask solo diners to order two servings. In November 2025 a restaurant sign refusing solo diners went viral in Korea, and most of the online reaction was against the owner. It is the restaurant's call, though, so:

- If the menu says "2인분 이상" (two servings or more), that is the rule.
- Ask at the door before sitting. Showing "1인분 돼요?" ("Can I order one serving?") in Papago works.

## Restaurant basics

- **Water is free**, and in some places you help yourself from a dispenser or a fridge by the wall. Refills of side dishes are free too (KTO).
- **Call bell.** Every table has a call button. KTO warns it may be louder than you expect.
- **Paying.** Many places now take your order and payment on a table tablet or a kiosk at the door. If you ordered from staff and no one brings the bill, take the slip from your table to the counter on the way out.
- **Cards** are accepted almost everywhere; see [Money in Korea](/money/).

## How much English to expect

Korea ranked 48th in the 2025 EF English Proficiency Index, with Seoul scoring higher than the national average. The UK government notes that taxi drivers "tend to speak little or no English", so keep your destination written in Korean.

What helps most:

- Papago, Naver's translator, for menus (point the camera) and short conversations.
- Naver Map, set to English, so you can show the Korean address on screen.
- **1330 Korea Travel Hotline** for anything more complicated (below).

## Safety, including for solo women

Korea is one of the safer places to travel. The US State Department rates it Level 1 ("exercise normal precautions") and calls it "a very safe country" for most visitors, and Canada's advice, updated in October 2026, says crime against foreigners is generally low.

The same governments flag the same risks, so take them as our short list:

- **Nightlife areas.** Canada says sexual assault and harassment "are not uncommon" around bars in places like Itaewon, Gangnam and Hongdae. Don't leave drinks unattended or accept drinks from new acquaintances.
- **People met on apps.** The US State Department says most reported sexual assaults on young women involve people met on social media or dating apps, and alcohol is often a factor.
- **Taxis.** Use licensed taxis or a taxi app such as Kakao T.
- **Pickpockets** in crowded markets and tourist areas.

The 1330 hotline is run by KTO. It covers Korean, English, Japanese, Chinese, Russian, Vietnamese, Thai and Malay/Indonesian, runs 7 a.m. to midnight every day, and can set up a three-way call with you and emergency services. Dial 1330 in Korea or +82-2-1330 from abroad. You can also chat through its web messenger, KakaoTalk, LINE, Facebook or WhatsApp; free online calls over wifi are on the [official 1330 page](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?vcontsId=140632&menuSn=454). What to say on each call is in our [emergency guide](/emergency/).

## Plugs and voltage

Korea uses 220V at 60Hz with a two-round-pin plug (the type usually listed as C or F). The US runs on 120V with flat pins, so you need an adapter. Most phone and laptop chargers say "100–240V" on the label and only need the adapter; a hair dryer or straightener that says 120V only will need a converter, or leave it at home. KTO says hotel front desks can often lend adapters, and they are sold at Incheon Airport convenience stores.

## Drinking age and ID

The legal age for buying alcohol and tobacco is set by birth year, not birthday: you count as an adult from 1 January of the year you turn 19. In 2026 that means anyone born in 2007 or earlier; in 2027, 2008 or earlier. You are legally required to carry ID in Korea, and a passport is the ID that works for visitors, so keep it on you.

## Smoking

All restaurants, cafés and bars have been no-smoking indoors since 2015. Outdoors in Seoul, smoking is banned within 10 metres of every subway exit, at bus stops, in city parks such as Namsan and Yeouido, and on some streets like Gangnam-daero. The fine is up to ₩100,000, and since 24 April 2026 the rules also cover liquid e-cigarettes made with synthetic nicotine. Look for signed smoking booths.

## Trash and recycling

Expect to carry your rubbish. Seoul removed street bins after the 1995 pay-per-bag waste system, when people started dumping household trash in them. The city has been adding bins back since 2023 for tourists, and had 7,142 in September 2026, still fewer than in 1995. Convenience stores and subway stations are your best bet, and where a bin has separate slots for recyclables, use the right one.

## Subway manners

- **Pink seats** are for pregnant women (two per car). Our advice: leave them empty, even when the train is crowded.
- **Seats at each end of the car** are for elderly and disabled passengers.
- **Keep quiet.** Talking softly and keeping phone calls short is the norm Seoul has asked for since its early etiquette campaigns.

## Before you go

1. Save 112, 119 and 1330 in your phone; from abroad 1330 is +82-2-1330.
2. Pack a two-round-pin adapter, and read the voltage on your chargers.
3. Install Papago and Naver Map, and keep your hotel's address in Korean.
4. Pick your first *honbap*: a noodle shop, a gukbap place or a kimbap shop.

Who doesn't need this page? Repeat visitors who already know the bell and the bins. If that's you, save the 1330 number and move on.
