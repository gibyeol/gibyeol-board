This page is practical help for finding care, not medical advice. If you are unsure how serious something is, call 1339 (below) or 119.

## Our call

| Situation | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A cold or headache at 11 p.m. | A 24-hour convenience store | Registered 24-hour stores can sell a short list of household medicines |
| You want a pharmacist's advice after hours | A late-night or holiday pharmacy, found on E-Gen | Local governments designate pharmacies open late, often until midnight or later |
| Fever or symptoms that won't settle after a few days | A neighborhood clinic (*uiwon*) | Clinics are where Koreans go for colds, skin problems and indigestion |
| You need English, Japanese or Chinese help and a planned visit | The international center at a big university hospital | They book foreign patients and can estimate costs first |
| Chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, heavy bleeding, a bad injury | Call 119 | That is the ambulance and fire number, 24 hours |
| Not sure which of these you need | Call 1339 | The government health line answers 24 hours and can link an interpreter |

The three to remember: convenience stores for small things at night, E-Gen to find what's open, and 119 for anything serious. We left out Korean traditional medicine and cosmetic clinics; they are a different trip.

## If you get sick on the trip: the scene

**Before you fly (five minutes).** Save 119 and 1339 in your phone. Pack the medicine you already take, in its labelled box. If an ingredient is controlled in Korea, apply to the MFDS before the trip and allow up to 10 business days.

**11 p.m., a headache.** At a 24-hour convenience store the medicine shelf has four kinds: fever and pain relievers, cold medicines, digestives and pain patches. One pack per product, and no pharmacist to ask.

**Next morning, still not right.** A neighborhood clinic, your passport, the full price at the desk, and an itemized receipt in English.

**The ending to plan for:** the receipts in one envelope for your insurer, and the rest of the trip.

## Numbers to save

- **119** is ambulance and fire. The UK government's advice is simply: "Call 119 and ask for an ambulance."
- **1339** is the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) call center: 24 hours, every day, free from a Korean number. It covers everyday health questions and infectious disease, and the KDCA's KakaoTalk channel offers one-to-one chat. From abroad it is +82-2-2633-1339 (charged).
- **1330** can join a three-way call with you and these services when language is the problem. Hours and languages are on our [Everyday Korea](/everyday/) page.

## Convenience-store medicine

Korea lets some convenience stores sell "safe household medicines" without a pharmacist under the [Pharmaceutical Affairs Act (Article 44-2)](https://www.law.go.kr/%EB%B2%95%EB%A0%B9/%EC%95%BD%EC%82%AC%EB%B2%95/%EC%A0%9C44%EC%A1%B0%EC%9D%982). The store has to be registered with the local government and open 24 hours, every day of the year.

What you will find is a short list in four groups: fever and pain relievers, cold medicines, digestives, and medicated pain patches. Thirteen products are designated, but two are no longer produced, so in practice there are 11 (Economist Korea, 16 November 2026). Things like diarrhea medicine and burn cream are not on the list. The government has talked about expanding it to as many as 20 products since July 2026; as of mid-November nothing had changed.

{{fig:health-2|The convenience-store medicine shelf and its rules}}

Two rules you will notice at the till:

- **One pack per product per purchase.** That is the sales rule, not the cashier being difficult.
- **No pharmacist.** The Korea Times put it plainly: these purchases happen without a pharmacist's advice. If symptoms continue, see a clinic.

Not every store stocks every product, and not every convenience store is 24-hour. More on chains and opening hours on our [convenience store](/convenience-store/) page.

## Pharmacies, including at night

Look for the character **약** ("medicine") on the sign. Pharmacies sell over-the-counter medicine with advice from the pharmacist, and fill prescriptions written by Korean clinics.

{{fig:health-3|The pharmacy sign, and three ways to find one open}}

Most close in the evening. To find one that is open:

- **E-Gen** ([e-gen.or.kr](https://www.e-gen.or.kr)), the National Emergency Medical Center's portal. Choose your area, pick pharmacy or hospital, then a date or holiday to see what is open. The Korean government recommends it for holidays.
- **Late-night pharmacies** designated by local governments typically open from 10 p.m. to midnight or into the early morning (Korea Times, March 2026).
- **Holiday pharmacies** post their weekend and holiday hours on [pharm114.or.kr](https://www.pharm114.or.kr), the pharmacists' holiday pharmacy site.
- **1339** can also point you to late-night pharmacies and after-hours clinics.

These are built for Korean users (the government's emergency app is Korean-only). Our honest advice: if map-reading a Korean government site at midnight sounds bad, call 1339 or ask your hotel front desk.

## Seeing a doctor as a tourist

**Clinic or hospital?** For a cold, skin trouble or an upset stomach, a small clinic (*uiwon*) is the normal choice. Hospitals and university hospitals are for scans, admission or surgery. Bring your passport.

**What it costs.** Unless you are enrolled in Korea's national health insurance, you pay the full price yourself. Naeil Shinmun (December 2025) put a typical clinic visit at roughly ₩30,000–60,000 without insurance, against ₩5,000–20,000 with it. The UK government says care is good but "can be expensive", and tells travelers to have insurance that covers treatment and medical evacuation. Keep every receipt and ask for an itemized one in English for your claim.

{{fig:health-4|A clinic visit without Korean insurance, step by step}}

**International centers.** Big Seoul hospitals have desks for foreign patients:

- **Seoul National University Hospital International Healthcare Center**: weekdays 9:00–17:30, for foreign patients without Korean national health insurance. Book by phone (+82-2-2072-0505, prioritized) or online.
- **Severance Hospital International Health Care Center**: interpreting in English, Japanese, Russian and Chinese. It will give an estimated cost before treatment on request; inpatient bills can go to overseas insurers directly, while most outpatients pay and claim later.

Our view: these centers make sense for a planned visit or anything beyond a cold. For a sore throat, a local clinic is faster.

## Bringing your own medicine

From 12 November 2026, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) and Korea Customs Service allow travelers to carry up to **six bottles** of medicine for their own treatment, or more if it is no more than **three months' supply** by the label. It must have been prescribed or bought legally.

Medicines with narcotic or psychotropic ingredients are different: you need MFDS approval **before** you fly, whatever your nationality. Apply on Drug Safety Korea (nedrug.mfds.go.kr); [the Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10851424) says to allow up to 10 business days. Check ingredients, not brand names: the Herald's example is dextromethorphan, found in some versions of DayQuil, which is a controlled psychotropic in Korea. The UK government also suggests carrying an English prescription.

{{fig:health-5|Which rule applies to your medicine}}

Who doesn't need this page? A healthy traveler on a short trip. Save 119 and 1339, pack the medicine you already use, and get on with the trip.
